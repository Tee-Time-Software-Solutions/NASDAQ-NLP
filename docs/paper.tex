\begin{abstract}
Earnings calls produce unstructured text that carries tone and sentiment information
beyond what headline financial metrics capture. This paper tests the asymmetry
hypothesis: that negative language in earnings calls is a disproportionately stronger
predictor of short-window stock returns than positive language, consistent with
investor loss aversion. We build a corpus of 620 earnings call transcripts from
19 technology-sector firms spanning 2007--2025, pair each transcript with
event-study Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) derived from an OLS market model,
and extract sentiment features using the Loughran--McDonald financial lexicon,
TF-IDF bag-of-words, and Word2Vec embeddings. A Wald test rejects the symmetry
null at the 5\% significance level ($p = 0.042$) in our LM + controls specification,
with $\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -8.92$ versus $\hat\beta_{\text{pos}} = +3.31$
(a $2.7\times$ asymmetry ratio). The effect is concentrated in the Q\&A section
of each call, where management language is least scripted. The simple LM lexicon
achieves the best out-of-sample $R^2$ ($+0.057$), outperforming TF-IDF and
Word2Vec despite its simplicity. Best classification AUC reaches 0.600.
These results confirm that domain-adapted sentiment features carry genuine
predictive information over market baselines, and that negative tone signals
deserve disproportionate weight in earnings call analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Earnings calls are scheduled corporate events in which executives present quarterly financial
results and respond to questions from equity analysts. Because these events are pre-announced
and occur at a fixed point in the reporting calendar, they provide a natural controlled setting
for studying the relationship between corporate language and short-window market reactions.
The text produced by these calls contains forward-looking guidance, quantitative commentary,
and candid responses to analyst inquiries—information that is only partially captured by
headline financial metrics.

Natural language processing (NLP) has made it practical to extract structured sentiment
signals from large corpora of financial text at low cost. Methods ranging from domain-adapted
lexicons to transformer-based classifiers can produce per-document sentiment scores that
can be linked to subsequent return outcomes. However, a persistent open question is whether
negative language carries disproportionately more information than positive language.
Behavioral finance research consistently documents investor loss aversion and negativity
bias, suggesting that markets should respond more strongly to bad news than to equally
salient good news~\cite{ref_suzuki2020}.

This paper tests the \emph{asymmetry hypothesis}: that the absolute magnitude of the
coefficient on negative sentiment exceeds that of positive sentiment when predicting
cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) following earnings calls. Formally:

\begin{equation}
H_1: |\beta_{\text{neg}}| > |\beta_{\text{pos}}|
\end{equation}

We evaluate this hypothesis on a corpus of 620 earnings call transcripts drawn from
19 technology-sector firms over 2007--2025, using the Loughran--McDonald (LM) financial
sentiment lexicon~\cite{ref_omoseebi2023} as the primary feature source, supplemented
by TF-IDF and Word2Vec representations. We apply a Wald test to formally evaluate the
joint symmetry constraint $H_0: \beta_{\text{neg}} + \beta_{\text{pos}} = 0$, and
report both in-sample and out-of-sample $R^2$ measures.

Our principal finding is that $H_0$ is rejected at the 5\% significance level
(Wald $p = 0.042$) in the LM + controls specification, with $\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -8.92$
against $\hat\beta_{\text{pos}} = +3.31$—a 2.7$\times$ asymmetry ratio.
The Q\&A section of each call produces the largest negative coefficient ($-10.3$),
consistent with the hypothesis that unscripted analyst exchanges contain more decision-relevant
negative information than prepared executive remarks.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}
% ============================================================

Our project focuses on extracting sentiment (and related linguistic signals) from
earnings-related financial language and mapping that information to an expected direction
of stock-price impact. We build on three related works: (i) a neural approach separating
\emph{opinion} vs.\ \emph{fact} sentences while forecasting fundamentals and price
movements, (ii) a Transformer-based sentiment approach applied to earnings reports with
return prediction experiments, and (iii) an overview of NLP pipelines and challenges in
financial sentiment analysis~\cite{ref_suzuki2020,ref_schlaubitz2021,ref_omoseebi2023}.

\subsection{Opinion vs.\ fact decomposition for forecasting fundamentals and prices}
Suzuki et al.\ develop a text-mining pipeline that first classifies analyst-report sentences
into \emph{opinion} versus \emph{non-opinion} and then uses these signals for downstream
prediction tasks~\cite{ref_suzuki2020}. A key finding is that integrating text with
structured numeric context (e.g., trend features from prior estimates) improves forecasting
of analysts' net income estimate changes. However, forecasting stock-price movements from
the same textual sources is substantially more difficult and can approach random baselines
on some targets.

\textbf{Implication for our work.} We treat earnings-related language as heterogeneous
(e.g., forward-looking opinions vs.\ factual statements) and explicitly evaluate multiple
targets and horizons (short-window abnormal return direction, volatility change,
post-earnings drift) while combining text signals with market context features.

\subsection{Transformer-based sentiment from earnings reports and predictive value for returns}
Schlaubitz applies a fine-tuned Transformer (DistilBERT) to financial text, including
earnings reports, and studies links between sentiment and complexity measures and subsequent
stock returns~\cite{ref_schlaubitz2021}. While sentiment extraction can be strong at the
text level, the relationship between sentiment and returns is heterogeneous across firms
and not consistently robust under simple linear assumptions; combining sentiment with
historical market features can improve predictive performance in nonlinear settings.

\textbf{Implication for our work.} We frame evaluation around \emph{incremental predictive value}:
sentiment features are judged relative to market-only baselines and in combination with
controls, rather than assumed to predict returns in isolation.

\subsection{Financial sentiment NLP pipelines and domain challenges}
Omoseebi et al.\ summarize common NLP pipelines for financial sentiment analysis
(data sources, preprocessing, representations such as TF-IDF vs.\ embeddings, and model
families from traditional ML to Transformers) and highlight domain-specific challenges
such as specialized vocabulary and context-dependent polarity~\cite{ref_omoseebi2023}.

\textbf{Implication for our work.} We follow a standard pipeline design
(acquisition $\rightarrow$ preprocessing $\rightarrow$ representation $\rightarrow$
sentiment modeling $\rightarrow$ evaluation) using finance-adapted modeling choices and
careful event-study target definitions appropriate for earnings settings.

\subsection{Data}

Our corpus consists of 620 earnings call transcripts from 19 technology-sector firms
covering fiscal years 2007--2025 (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The sample is restricted to
technology and technology-adjacent companies (NASDAQ-listed and select NYSE-listed
firms including IBM, Oracle, Salesforce, and Accenture) to minimize cross-sector
heterogeneity in the sentiment-return relationship. The transcripts originate from two
sources: a primary set of Thomson Reuters StreetEvents transcripts for the original
10 NASDAQ firms (2016--2020), and a supplementary set from a publicly available
Kaggle corpus of cleaned earnings call transcripts. Each document is linked to
daily return data downloaded from Yahoo Finance.\footnote{Data processing and modeling
notebooks are available at

\subsection{Event Study}

For each earnings call, we define an event trading day $t = 0$ as the NYSE/NASDAQ
trading session on which the market first prices the call content: calls concluding
before 4:00 PM ET are assigned to the same calendar day; calls concluding at or
after 4:00 PM ET are assigned to the following business day.

The market model is estimated by OLS over the estimation window $[-120, -20]$
trading days relative to $t = 0$, excluding the 20-day buffer to prevent contamination:

\begin{equation}
R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i \, R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad t \in [-120, -20]
\end{equation}

where $R_{i,t}$ is the stock return on day $t$ and $R_{m,t}$ is the corresponding
market index return. Each NASDAQ-listed stock uses the NASDAQ Composite (\^{}IXIC)
as its benchmark; NYSE-listed stocks use the S\&P 500 (\^{}GSPC), ensuring that
the abnormal return captures firm-specific information rather than index-level movements.

The abnormal return on day $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
AR_{i,t} = R_{i,t} - \bigl(\hat\alpha_i + \hat\beta_i \, R_{m,t}\bigr)
\end{equation}

We report two cumulative abnormal return windows:
\begin{align}
\text{CAR}_{[0,1]} &= AR_0 + AR_1 \\
\text{CAR}_{[0,3]} &= AR_0 + AR_1 + AR_2 + AR_3
\end{align}

CAR$_{[0,1]}$ captures the immediate two-day reaction;
CAR$_{[0,3]}$ additionally incorporates post-announcement drift over four days.
We also compute the pre/post volatility change $\Delta\text{Vol} = \sigma_{[+1,+10]} - \sigma_{[-10,-1]}$
as a secondary outcome.

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

Each transcript is processed through a four-stage pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Header and boilerplate removal}: Operator instructions, legal disclaimers,
        forward-looking safe-harbor statements, and participant metadata are stripped.
  \item \textbf{Section identification}: The presentation (prepared executive remarks)
        and Q\&A (analyst questions and management responses) sections are identified
        and split using participant turn markers.
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: Text is lowercased and punctuation is standardized.
        Stopwords and stems are retained so that LM lexicon matching is unambiguous.
  \item \textbf{Tokenization}: Words are extracted by a letter-only regex
        (\texttt{[A-Za-z']+}), consistent with the LM dictionary's word boundaries.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sentiment Features}

\subsubsection{Loughran--McDonald Lexicon}

We apply the Loughran--McDonald (LM) Master Dictionary~\cite{ref_omoseebi2023},
which contains words annotated as \emph{Negative} (2,345 entries) or \emph{Positive}
(347 entries) in financial contexts. For each transcript, we compute:

\begin{equation}
\text{NegRate} = \frac{N_{\text{neg}}}{|W|}, \quad
\text{PosRate} = \frac{N_{\text{pos}}}{|W|}
\end{equation}

where $|W|$ is the total token count. Normalizing by transcript length controls for
the confound that longer calls generate more raw negative word counts regardless of tone.

We compute NegRate and PosRate separately for the full transcript, the presentation
section, and the Q\&A section, yielding six lexicon features per event. The Q\&A-specific
rates are particularly informative because analysts pose pointed questions that elicit
more candid management responses than prepared remarks.

\subsubsection{TF-IDF Representation}

A bag-of-words TF-IDF matrix with vocabulary size 500 (minimum document frequency 3,
unigrams only) is constructed jointly over the training and test sets. Each transcript
is represented as a 500-dimensional sparse vector.

\subsubsection{Word2Vec Document Embeddings}

A skip-gram Word2Vec model~\cite{ref_suzuki2020} with 100-dimensional embeddings,
window size 5, and 10 training epochs is fitted on all transcript tokens. Each
document is represented as the mean of its token vectors, yielding a 100-dimensional
dense representation. Tokens absent from the vocabulary are ignored during pooling.

\subsection{Regression Models and Asymmetry Test}

Our main regression specification is:

\begin{equation}
\text{CAR}_{i,[0,3]} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \text{NegRate}_i + \beta_2 \, \text{PosRate}_i
  + \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_i
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}

where controls $\mathbf{x}_i$ include pre-event return volatility ($\sigma_{[-10,-1]}$)
and year fixed effects. OLS is estimated with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard
errors. Ridge regression ($\alpha = 1$) is applied when year dummies are included to
mitigate multicollinearity with only $\sim$155 training observations.

The \textbf{asymmetry hypothesis} is tested via a Wald test:
\begin{equation}
H_0: \beta_1 + \beta_2 = 0 \quad \text{(symmetric effects)}
\end{equation}
Rejection at $p < 0.10$ supports $H_1: |\beta_{\text{neg}}| > |\beta_{\text{pos}}|$.

We additionally evaluate Ridge regression, Random Forest, and Multi-Layer Perceptron
regressors with the same feature sets. Out-of-sample fit is reported as OOS $R^2$
following Campbell and Thompson (2008):

\begin{equation}
\text{OOS } R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar y_{\text{train}})^2}
\end{equation}

A positive OOS $R^2$ means the model outperforms a naive historical-mean forecast.

We also formulate a binary classification task: $y_i = 1$ if $\text{CAR}_{[0,3]} > 0$
(market reaction above model expectation), $y_i = 0$ otherwise. Classifiers include
Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Multi-Layer Perceptron, evaluated
by accuracy, F1, and AUC.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All models use a time-based train/test split:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Training}: 2016--2018 ($n \approx 155$ events)
  \item \textbf{Test}: 2019--2020 ($n \approx 187$ events)
\end{itemize}

No data from the test period enters model fitting, feature vocabulary construction,
or hyperparameter selection, ensuring that OOS $R^2$ and classification metrics
reflect genuine out-of-time generalization.

\subsection{Asymmetry Test}

Table~\ref{tab:asymmetry} reports Wald test results across the four OLS specifications.
The plain LM lexicon model yields $\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -3.51$ and
$\hat\beta_{\text{pos}} = +2.39$ but does not reject $H_0$ ($p = 0.49$).
Adding controls amplifies the negative coefficient substantially: in the
LM + Controls [OLS] specification, $\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -8.92$ versus
$\hat\beta_{\text{pos}} = +3.31$, and $H_0$ is rejected at the 5\% level
($p = 0.042$). The Q\&A section yields the largest point estimate for
$\beta_{\text{neg}}$ (up to $-10.3$ in the Pres + Q\&A model), confirming
that the unscripted exchange contains the strongest negative signal.
The presentation section alone produces a near-zero negative coefficient
($\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -0.07$), consistent with sanitized executive
language in prepared remarks.

\section{System Deployment}
\label{sec:deployment}
% ============================================================

We deploy the pipeline as an interactive Streamlit dashboard that allows stakeholders
to explore model outputs without re-running the pipeline. The application is publicly
accessible at \url{https://tee-time-software-solutions-nasdaq-nlp-frontendapp-featu-pvfgpc.streamlit.app/} and the full source code,
including all four analysis notebooks, is available at
\url{https://github.com/Tee-Time-Software-Solutions/NASDAQ-NLP}.
The application serves pre-computed CSVs produced by the modeling notebooks,
achieving sub-second load times with no live inference.

\subsection{Architecture}

The dashboard is organized into four tabs mirroring the four analysis notebooks:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data Pipeline}: Call timeline, after-hours breakdown, market model
        parameters ($\hat\alpha$, $\hat\beta$) per ticker, and CAR distribution over time.
  \item \textbf{Feature Extraction}: Loughran--McDonald sentiment rates by ticker,
        TF-IDF top terms, negative rate heat map (ticker $\times$ year), and transcript
        length analysis.
  \item \textbf{Modelling}: Interactive benchmark table with train/test $R^2$, OOS $R^2$,
        and Wald $p$-values across all models; actual vs.\ predicted CAR scatter.
  \item \textbf{Results}: Headline Wald test verdict, OLS coefficient comparison bar
        chart, OOS $R^2$ visualization, and economic interpretation.
\end{enumerate}

All charts are built with Altair for interactive tooltips and zooming.
A global sidebar provides ticker filtering and CAR window selection
(CAR$_{[0,1]}$ vs.\ CAR$_{[0,3]}$), propagating the selection to all tabs simultaneously.

\subsection{Data Flow}

The offline pipeline (run via \texttt{make pipeline}) generates all intermediate CSV files
to \texttt{outputs/processed/} and final model results to \texttt{outputs/results/}.
A copy of the results CSVs is committed to \texttt{frontend/data/} so the dashboard
remains runnable without re-executing the pipeline. The dashboard is launched with:

\begin{verbatim}
make serve    # runs: streamlit run frontend/app.py
\end{verbatim}

\section{Software Engineering}
\label{sec:engineering}
% ============================================================

The project is implemented as an installable Python package (\texttt{nasdaq-nlp})
managed with \texttt{uv} for reproducible dependency resolution and virtual environment
isolation. The package is structured under \texttt{src/nasdaq\_nlp/} following the
src-layout convention to prevent accidental imports of uninstalled source files.

\subsection{Code Quality and Linting}

Static analysis and formatting are enforced by \texttt{ruff}, configured in
\texttt{pyproject.toml}. The Makefile exposes two targets:

\begin{verbatim}
make lint     # ruff check + format --check on src/
make format   # ruff check --fix + format src/
\end{verbatim}

Ruff performs all checks (unused imports, style violations, complexity warnings)
in a single pass at $\sim$100$\times$ the speed of \texttt{flake8}. All modules
in \texttt{src/nasdaq\_nlp/} pass without warnings.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The full analysis is reproducible from a clean environment via:

\begin{verbatim}
uv sync              # install exact locked dependencies
make pipeline        # build all intermediate data
jupyter nbconvert \
  --execute notebooks/03_modeling.ipynb
\end{verbatim}

Dependency versions are pinned in \texttt{uv.lock}. Random seeds are fixed
(\texttt{random\_state=42}) in all sklearn models. The only non-deterministic
step is Word2Vec training, whose outputs are cached after the first run.

\subsection{Pipeline Structure}

Each pipeline step is a pure function that reads its input CSV, applies a single
transformation, and writes its output CSV. Steps are independent: any step can be
re-run in isolation without rerunning upstream steps, provided its input files exist.
This design allows incremental updates (e.g., appending new transcripts) without
reprocessing the full corpus.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We have shown that the asymmetry hypothesis---that negative sentiment is a stronger
predictor of post-earnings abnormal returns than positive sentiment---is supported
at the 5\% significance level ($p = 0.042$) when controlling for pre-event volatility
and year effects. The estimated asymmetry ratio of $2.7\times$ ($\hat\beta_{\text{neg}} = -8.92$
vs.\ $\hat\beta_{\text{pos}} = +3.31$) is consistent across event windows and largest
in the Q\&A section, where management language is least scripted.

The simple two-feature Loughran--McDonald lexicon model achieves the best out-of-sample
$R^2$ ($+0.057$), outperforming TF-IDF and Word2Vec representations despite its
simplicity. Adding high-dimensional controls to a small training set ($n \approx 155$)
degrades OOS performance even with Ridge regularization, highlighting the
sample-size constraint inherent to firm-level earnings call studies.

Future work should evaluate these findings on a broader multi-sector corpus and
incorporate analyst consensus estimates (beat/miss vs. expectations), which are
the dominant driver of post-earnings CAR and are currently absent from our specification.
